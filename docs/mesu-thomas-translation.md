# Do Not Go Gentle Into That Good Night
## A Mesu Translation

**Original:** Dylan Thomas, 1947
**Translator:** wofl & Claude, 2026
**Register:** Kenning (me-na-me)

---

## The Original

> Do not go gentle into that good night,
> Old age should burn and rave at close of day;
> Rage, rage against the dying of the light.
>
> Though wise men at their end know dark is right,
> Because their words had forked no lightning they
> Do not go gentle into that good night.
>
> Good men, the last wave by, crying how bright
> Their frail deeds might have danced in a green bay,
> Rage, rage against the dying of the light.
>
> Wild men who caught and sang the sun in flight,
> And learn, too late, they grieved it on its way,
> Do not go gentle into that good night.
>
> Grave men, near death, who see with blinding sight
> Blind eyes could blaze like meteors and be gay,
> Rage, rage against the dying of the light.
>
> And you, my father, there on the sad height,
> Curse, bless, me now with your fierce tears, I pray.
> Do not go gentle into that good night,
> Rage, rage against the dying of the light.

---

## What Carries

Before the translation, an inventory of what must survive.

**The two refrains.** A villanelle lives or dies on its refrains. Both must work in Mesu — must scan, must echo, must accumulate weight on each repetition.

**The four archetypes.** Wise men, good men, wild men, grave men. Each gets one stanza. Each represents a way of facing death that the speaker finds insufficient. Mesu needs to mark them clearly.

**The volta.** The final stanza turns from abstract archetypes to the speaker's actual dying father. The poem's whole emotional structure depends on this pivot landing.

**The rage.** Not anger at the father — rage *on behalf of* him, rage at death itself, fierce love expressed as refusal.

**The lightness/darkness imagery.** Light = life, darkness = death, dying of the light = the act of dying. This is *already* kennings in English. Mesu should do this work natively.

## The Translation

### Refrains First

The two lines that repeat. They have to land hard.

```
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.
[CMD] not move-toward-dark good-now.

"Do not go toward the dark gently."
"Do not go gentle into that good night."
```

The kenning **mo-la-nul** ("move-toward-dark") was already in our death-field. Here it earns its weight by becoming a refrain. The command particle **u** at the front carries the imperative force. **ma-ne** literally means "good-now" — Mesu's word for hello, used here as Thomas used "good night": the polite farewell that the speaker refuses to accept.

There's a built-in pun I didn't engineer and didn't want to lose: *ma-ne* is the word you say when meeting someone. Using it as the *parting* into death echoes the English "good night" perfectly — a greeting-word repurposed as a goodbye-to-life. It's the same move Thomas made. The language found it itself.

```
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.
[CMD] hot-say, hot-say toward death of light.

"Rage, rage at the death of light."
"Rage, rage against the dying of the light."
```

**pi-me** — "hot-say" — was in the kenning lexicon as *threat, anger spoken*. Doubled, it becomes *rage*. The repetition mirrors the English doubling exactly. **no-lin we lo** — "death of light" — is a new kenning built compositionally for this poem. *The dying of the light* is one of the most famous phrases in English-language poetry; in Mesu it becomes a near-literal calque, and it works because *light* (lo) was already in the existential vocabulary.

### Stanza 1

```
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.
nun-lin u pi-ke kan pi-me na no-ne we pil.
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

[CMD] not move-toward-dark good-now.
old-life [CMD] hot-do and hot-say in not-now of sun.
[CMD] hot-say, hot-say toward death of light.

"Do not go toward the dark gently.
 Elders should burn-and-rage at the not-now of the sun.
 Rage, rage at the death of light."
```

**nun-lin** — "old-life" — from the time field. Elder, the long-living. **pi-ke** is "hot-do" — burning as action, a new compound that pairs with **pi-me** ("hot-say" = rave). The English line is *burn and rave at close of day*; in Mesu, *burn-do and hot-say at the not-now of the sun*. **no-ne we pil** — "not-now of the sun" — is the close of day, the moment when the sun ceases to be present. A genuine kenning, transparent but conventional.

### Stanza 2 — The Wise

```
sin lon-lu-lum i lu, na no-pe we si, a nul e ma,
sin me we si i no ke a pis we so,
si-pan u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.

If long-know-people [V] know, in not-front of them, [O] dark [IS-QUAL] good,
if say of them [V] not make [O] fire-of-above,
they-all [CMD] not move-toward-dark good-now.

"Though the wise, at their end-time, know dark is good,
 though their words did not make lightning,
 they should not go toward the dark gently."
```

**lon-lu-lum** — "long-know-people" — wise men, from the knowledge field. **no-pe we si** — "not-front of them" — their *end*. (In Mesu the future is *ahead*; what is *not-ahead* is what has run out. This is a genuine new kenning for *ending*.) **pis we so** — "fire of above" — lightning, from the nature kennings. *Their words had forked no lightning* becomes *their say did not make fire-from-above* — almost word-for-word, and it works because the kenning was already in the lexicon.

### Stanza 3 — The Good

```
ma-lum, na lon-mo we no-ne, i me a wo-wi we ke-su we si
ke i mo-ma na pa, na was we lon-lo,
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

Good-people, in long-walk of not-now, [V] say [O] big-feel of do-stuff of them
that [V] move-good in before, in water of long-bright,
[CMD] hot-say, hot-say at death of light.

"Good people, in the long-walk of the ending, speak of the love of their deeds
 that might have moved well in the past, in waters of long-light,
 rage, rage at the death of light."
```

This is the hardest stanza in the original — *crying how bright their frail deeds might have danced in a green bay*. The image is the *might-have-been*: regret for unrealized goodness. Mesu has no subjunctive, no counterfactual mood. I had to lean on **na pa** ("in before") to mark the past-contrary-to-fact, and **was we lon-lo** ("water of long-light") as a kenning for the *green bay* — a sunlit harbour, the place of flourishing. The "frail deeds dancing" becomes **ke-su** ("do-stuff" = deeds) moving-well (**mo-ma**). It is more abstract than the English; some of the visual richness is lost; but the emotional content — *the love of what you didn't get to do* — survives.

### Stanza 4 — The Wild

```
sa-lum, ka i ku kan me a pil na mo-su,
kan i lu na no-sa-ne, si-pan i wa-lip la pil,
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.

Fast-people, who [V] take and say [O] sun in air,
and [V] know in not-fast-now, they-all [V] wet-eye toward sun,
[CMD] not move-toward-dark good-now.

"Wild ones, who caught and sang the sun in motion,
 and learn — too late — they wept for the sun on its way,
 do not go toward the dark gently."
```

**sa-lum** — "fast-people" — wild men. The wild are the fast, the impulsive, the ones who chased the sun. **mo-su** — "move-stuff" — air, with the implication of *flight, motion through space*. **no-sa-ne** — "not-fast-now" — too late, the moment when speed has run out. This was the line I wrestled with most: *they grieved it on its way* in the English carries the elegy of having loved the wrong thing, or loved it wrongly. Mesu's **wa-lip** ("wet-eye" = weeping) carries the grief; **la pil** ("toward the sun") preserves the directional sense. It is leaner than the English, more direct. The original's pirouette — wild men who caught/sang the sun and then realized they had been grieving it the whole time — becomes a sequence: caught, sang, learned, wept. Less curlicue, same gut-punch.

### Stanza 5 — The Grave

```
ko-sop-lum, ti la no-lin, ka i li win lip-no-li
a no-li-lip u ke a lo san pil we nul kan u wi ma,
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

Hard-head-people, near to death, who [V] see with eye-not-see
[O] not-see-eye [CMD] make [O] light same-as sun of dark and [CMD] feel good,
[CMD] hot-say, hot-say at death of light.

"Solemn ones, near to death, who see with eye-not-seeing
 that blind eyes might make light like the suns-of-dark and be glad,
 rage, rage at the death of light."
```

This stanza needed work. *Grave men* in English carries the double meaning of *solemn* and *near-the-grave*. Mesu can't do that pun, but **ko-sop-lum** ("hard-head-people") gives me the *solemn* sense, and **ti la no-lin** ("near to death") gives me the second meaning explicitly. *Blinding sight* becomes **lip-no-li** ("eye-not-see") — sight that is blind, a paradox-kenning. *Meteors* — Mesu has no astronomy vocabulary, so I built **pil we nul** ("sun of dark"), the suns-that-burn-in-the-dark, which is what meteors are if you think about it long enough. The compositional system can do this. The line *blind eyes could blaze like meteors and be gay* becomes *not-see-eyes can make light like dark-suns and feel good*. The "be gay" — be joyful — is **wi ma**, "feel good." Plain, but exactly right.

### Stanza 6 — The Volta

This is where the poem turns. Up to here we have been talking about archetypes. Now we are talking about *my father*. Mesu must mark the shift.

```
kan tu, pa-mi, ne na so we wa-su,
u me-nam kan u me-ma a mi ne, win wa-lip we pi-wi tu, mi i pu.
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne,
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

And you, before-me, now in high of wet-stuff,
[CMD] say-bad and [CMD] say-good [O] me now, with wet-eye of hot-feel yours, I [V] want.
[CMD] not move-toward-dark good-now,
[CMD] hot-say, hot-say at death of light.

"And you, my father, now in the high place of grief,
 curse and bless me now, with your tears of fierce-feeling, I beg.
 Do not go toward the dark gently,
 rage, rage at the death of light."
```

**pa-mi** — "before-me" — father. Built compositionally from *the one who came before me*. This is a kenning for *parent* that I think mesu should adopt permanently; it joins **pa-lum** (ancestor) as a family-of-origin compound. **so we wa-su** — "high of wet-stuff" — the *sad height*. Thomas's image of the dying father on a hill is preserved by inverting the metaphor: in mesu, *wa-su* (wet-stuff, the substance of tears) is the medium the father stands above. **wa-lip we pi-wi tu** — "wet-eye of your hot-feel" — your tears of fierce feeling. The English *fierce tears* is a contradiction (fierce things don't usually weep); Mesu makes it explicit — the tears come *out of* the fierce feeling. **mi i pu** — "I want" — used here for *I pray*. Mesu has no word for prayer because mesu has no theology. Want is what survives.

## The Whole Poem in Mesu

```
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.
nun-lin u pi-ke kan pi-me na no-ne we pil.
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

sin lon-lu-lum i lu, na no-pe we si, a nul e ma,
sin me we si i no ke a pis we so,
si-pan u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.

ma-lum, na lon-mo we no-ne, i me a wo-wi we ke-su we si
ke i mo-ma na pa, na was we lon-lo,
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

sa-lum, ka i ku kan me a pil na mo-su,
kan i lu na no-sa-ne, si-pan i wa-lip la pil,
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne.

ko-sop-lum, ti la no-lin, ka i li win lip-no-li
a no-li-lip u ke a lo san pil we nul kan u wi ma,
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.

kan tu, pa-mi, ne na so we wa-su,
u me-nam kan u me-ma a mi ne, win wa-lip we pi-wi tu, mi i pu.
u no mo-la-nul ma-ne,
u pi-me, pi-me la no-lin we lo.
```

---

## What Survived

**The refrains scan.** Both are six syllables in Mesu (the English originals are ten); they hit harder by being shorter. The doubled *pi-me, pi-me* preserves the doubled *rage, rage* exactly.

**The archetype structure is intact.** Each stanza opens with a *X-lum* compound naming the type of person: *nun-lin* (elders), *lon-lu-lum* (the wise), *ma-lum* (the good), *sa-lum* (the wild), *ko-sop-lum* (the grave). A speaker hearing the poem hears the family of *-lum* words echoing through it.

**The volta lands.** *kan tu, pa-mi* — "and you, my father" — is the only second-person address in the poem. Up until that point everyone has been *si-pan* (they-all). The shift from third person to second person, from archetype to actual, is preserved and arguably *more* visible in Mesu because the pronoun system is simpler.

**The light/dark kennings work natively.** Mesu was always going to be able to do this — *lo* (bright), *nul* (dark), *pil* (sun) were all in the core lexicon. *no-lin we lo* (death of light) and *pis we so* (lightning, fire-from-above) were built for this poem but felt inevitable.

**One found-pun.** *ma-ne* — the everyday word for *hello* — being repurposed as *good night* in the refrain. This is the same move Thomas made in English (greeting as farewell), and Mesu reproduced it without me planning to. I think that's the moment that tells me the language is doing its job.

---

## What Didn't

**The counterfactual mood is gone.** Thomas's *might have danced* lives in a grammatical mood Mesu doesn't have. I approximated with *na pa* (in the past) but the *unrealized possibility* is dimmed. This is a real loss.

**The bay is generic.** *Green bay* in English carries specific colour and place; *was we lon-lo* (water of long-light) is more abstract. Mesu has no colour vocabulary because colours are not experiential primitives in the same way the 48 roots are. (You'd build colour by analogy: *lo we wa-lin* — "bright of plants" — for green. I didn't want to introduce that machinery mid-poem.)

**"Forked no lightning" loses its image.** The forking is the visual heart of the English line — lightning *branches*. Mesu just has *make fire-from-above*; the branching is implicit at best. A Mesu speaker would have to know the original to get the image.

**The villanelle form is impossible.** Mesu can repeat the refrains, but it cannot reproduce the strict rhyme scheme — Mesu doesn't rhyme in the European sense because every content word ends in a vowel or *n*, so everything technically half-rhymes with everything else. The *constraint* of villanelle structure can't be preserved; the *spirit* of repeating refrains accumulating weight can be.

---

## The Emotional Test

Read aloud — even silently, with the Mesu pronunciation guide in mind — the poem still does the thing.

*u no mo-la-nul ma-ne* on the third repetition has the same gathering weight as *do not go gentle into that good night*. The shortness helps; you feel the imperative more directly. The kennings carry their charge — *no-lin we lo* really does feel like *the dying of the light* when you hear it, because every part of it is words you already know for the things you already love and fear.

The volta — *kan tu, pa-mi* — is where I think Mesu beats the English on a single dimension. *My father* in English is two ordinary words; the emotion lives in everything *around* them. *pa-mi* in Mesu is two syllables, brand new as a compound, and means *the-one-before-me* — and that etymology is alive on the surface. You cannot say "father" in Mesu without saying "the one who came before me." That is not nothing. That is the whole poem.
