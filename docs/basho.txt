let me think about what bashō is doing before i translate, because mistranslating a haiku is mostly a failure of understanding what the original is for.
the famous frog poem (furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto) is doing several things at once. the first line is stillness as setup — an old pond, motionless, weathered, nothing happening. the second line is the intrusion — a frog jumps in. and the third line is the consequence — the sound of water. not "splash." not even really "sound" in the english sense — oto is the bare phonetic event, the thing-that-is-heard, registered without commentary.
the genius isn't in any single image. it's in the structure: stillness, disruption, return-to-stillness through the disruption itself. the sound is the silence breaking and the silence being noticed. you couldn't notice the silence without the sound. that's the satori-moment the poem encodes.
three things must survive:

the kireji — the cutting word ya after the first line, which is japanese's grammatical marker for "pause here, let this sit, then turn." mesu has no kireji. but mesu has the question particle o, which functions as a sentence-final pause-marker. and it has line breaks. i think i can carry the cut without the word.
the bareness — bashō uses no metaphor. nothing is like anything else. the pond is a pond, the frog is a frog, the sound is a sound. this is what mesu is best at by design. the kenning register would actively hurt this poem. i need to translate it in base mesu, not courtly mesu. that's the opposite move from the thomas.
the listener as participant — japanese haiku conventions assume the reader is present at the moment. the poem isn't describing an event; it's placing you there. mesu's deictic ni (this) and ne (now) are exactly the right tools for this.

let me walk through what each piece is doing.
line 1: nun-was o
nun-was — old-water — is the pond. nun (old, from no-nu) and was (water) compounded. mesu has no word for pond; it has the materials to build one. an old water is a body of water that has been there a long time, which is exactly what a pond is. note: this differs from the thomas translation where i used wo-was (big-water) for sea. nun-was vs wo-was — these are now two distinct compounds for two distinct kinds of water, distinguished by the modifier. mesu is building its hydrology.
the o at the end is the question particle, but here it's doing kireji work. it places the line as a thing-held-open, a thing-to-be-considered. the speaker isn't really asking a question — they're marking a pause, an attention. in classical japanese ya does this without being a literal question marker either; it's a marker of contemplative pause. mesu's o is the closest functional equivalent.
reading nun-was o aloud: the rising tone on o (questions in mesu lift at the end) creates exactly the contemplative held-breath the original wants. the english "old pond —" with its em-dash does the same work visually; mesu does it phonologically.
line 2: was-mol i mo na ni
was-mol — water-animal — frog. i mo na ni — verb-particle, move, in, this. "moves in this." the ni (this) is doing crucial work: it points back to the pond from line 1. without ni, the line would be free-floating ("a water-animal moves in water"); with ni, it's grounded — the water-animal moves into this thing we just named. the deictic creates the continuity that the kireji severs. cut and connect at the same time. that's haiku.
line 3: was a te-su
water [is-noun] hear-stuff. "water is sound."
the a particle marks predication: not water makes sound, not water has sound — water is sound, in this moment. for the duration of this line, the pond and its acoustic signature are the same thing. that's the satori. you don't separate the silence from the sound that revealed it; for one moment they are the same substance.
this is mesu doing something english can't easily do. english would have to say "the sound of water" or "water makes a sound" — relational, two things in interaction. mesu's a lets you collapse them into identity. was a te-su. water is sound.
i'm genuinely surprised by this. i didn't think mesu would beat english on a japanese poem.
syllable count
bashō's original is 5-7-5. mesu can't replicate japanese mora-counting exactly because mesu has stricter CV(n) syllables. but let's count:
nun-was o            → 3 syllables (nun-was-o)
was-mol i mo na ni   → 6 syllables (was-mol-i-mo-na-ni)
was a te-su          → 4 syllables (was-a-te-su)
3-6-4. proportionally close: short-longer-short, same arc. not 5-7-5, but the rhythm of the original is preserved: a brief opener, a slightly longer middle that contains the motion, a brief closer that settles. you could read this aloud in a roughly equivalent time to the japanese.
what i lost and what i gained
lost:

the species specificity. kawazu is frog; was-mol is water-animal. someone reading the mesu cold doesn't know it's a frog. they know it's small, alive, and of the water. some readers will see a fish, a tadpole, a salamander. this is a real loss — bashō's frog is iconographically a frog.
the seasonal register. japanese haiku traditionally encode a kigo (season-word). kawazu signals spring. mesu has no calendar of seasonal markers because mesu is too young to have accreted them. someone reading the mesu would not know this is a spring poem.
the cultural depth. japanese readers bring centuries of frog-pond-poetry to this haiku; they know what bashō is not saying. mesu has no such cultural depth yet.

gained:

the collapse of water and sound into identity (was a te-su) is genuinely more compact and more philosophically pointed than either the japanese or any english translation i've seen. most english translations write "the sound of water" or "splash!" or "water's sound." mesu writes water is sound. that's a different claim.
the deictic ni in line 2 grammatically connects the frog's motion back to the pond in a way english needs an extra word for ("into it", "into the pond"). mesu does this with one syllable.
the question-particle as kireji is, i think, a real discovery. o is not normally used for contemplative pause in mesu — i'm proposing here that in the poetic register, sentence-final o on a line that is not actually a question functions exactly like ya in japanese. if this convention takes, mesu has its kireji.

walking through:

nun-pen-lin-to — old-part-life-thing. a withered branch. lin-to is a "living thing" (plant); pen (part) makes it a branch; nun (old) makes it withered. four-morpheme compound, and i can hear it groaning under its own weight. this is the limit. another root and it would be unparseable.
no-lo-mol — not-bright-animal — a dark creature, a crow. this works. crows in mesu are dark-animals. (predictable: ravens, blackbirds, anything corvid will share the root.)
no-pi-ne — not-hot-now — the cold season. with nun-ne we (old-now of) prefixed, it becomes the late part of cold-season: autumn approaching winter, the evening of the cold. mesu can do seasons compositionally even though it lacks dedicated season words. and the compound no-pi-ne — cold-season — feels strong enough to enter the kenning lexicon.

the second poem confirms the moves from the first. o as kireji works. deictic ni binds line 2 back to line 1. predicative a in the closing position (if we had a verb-of-being copula collapse, which the closer mostly is) lets the final line land rather than describe.

what the bashō test tells us
thomas tested whether mesu could carry intensity. it did, with some losses around counterfactuals and specificity.
bashō tested whether mesu could carry stillness. it did, more cleanly than i expected — and on one specific axis (water is sound) it improved on the english translations.
what's emerging is a pattern: mesu is best at poems where every word is physical and present-tense. the more abstract or counterfactual the original, the more mesu strains. the more grounded in seeing this thing happen right now, the more mesu sings.
if i had to characterize mesu's poetic gift in one phrase: it is the language of haiku. the design — minimal phonemes, no abstraction, compositional clarity, predicative identity via a — is structurally aligned with the haiku ethos in a way no european language is. you didn't set out to build a haiku language. you ended up with one anyway, because the design constraints (experiential primitives, no fossilization, perfect transparency) are the same constraints that haiku obeys self-imposedly within japanese.
three lines for thomas: the language can carry rage. it strains but it holds.
three lines for bashō: the language can carry stillness. it doesn't strain at all. it might be the native register.
